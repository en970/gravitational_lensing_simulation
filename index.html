How it works

How it works

A mass placed between an observer and a distant light source bends the paths of the light rays that pass near it. The source appears displaced, stretched into arcs, and in some cases duplicated. This is gravitational lensing, and it is a prediction of general relativity that has been measured since 1919.

The deflection

For a light ray passing at a distance r from a point mass M, the deflection angle in the weak-field limit is

θ = 4GM / c2r

This is Einstein's thin-lens approximation: the whole deflection is treated as happening in a single plane, which is a good description when the lens is much smaller than the distances involved. It is twice the value Newtonian gravity predicts for a corpuscle of light, and the factor of two is what the 1919 eclipse measurement confirmed.

What the simulation computes

The image is built by working backwards. For each pixel on the screen, at an angular distance θ from the centre of the lens, the simulation asks where in the background field that light must have come from. Writing θE for the Einstein radius, the source-plane position β is

β = θ − θE2 / θ

The pixel then takes the brightness of the background at β. Because a range of source positions is compressed into a smaller range of image positions, the background is also magnified, by

μ = | θ / β |

Where β passes through zero, μ diverges and the source is smeared into a complete ring — the Einstein ring. In the simulation the magnification is clamped to a factor of four, so the ring stays within the display range rather than saturating.

The dark centre

Two regions near the centre are handled separately. Inside the Schwarzschild radius rs no light escapes, so those pixels are black. Between rs and 1.5 rs lies the photon sphere, where light can orbit; it is darkened rather than removed. The thin white circle marks rs at the current mass.

Where the approximation ends

The thin-lens formula is a weak-field result, and this simulation applies it right up to the horizon, where it no longer holds. Close to rs a correct treatment requires integrating null geodesics in the Schwarzschild metric, which produces higher-order images and an Einstein ring structure the thin lens cannot reproduce. What is shown here is the weak-field picture, drawn everywhere.

Implementation

The calculation is per-pixel and order-independent, so it runs as a WebGL2 fragment shader: one pass, no geometry, at display resolution. The background field — stars, spiral galaxies, clusters and nebulae — is generated once from a fixed seed and uploaded as a single-channel texture, then sampled bilinearly through the deflection.

The original Python version performs the same computation with NumPy and pygame on the CPU. The equations, constants and background layout are unchanged between the two.
